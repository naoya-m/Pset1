1. WHAT THE DATA SUPPORTS, RECOMMENDATION BY RECOMMENDATION
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

R1. "Start from a flat 200 yen and aim to replace about a third of the tourism
     budget."

What the data carries: both ends of the comparison are measured values rather
than estimates. The tax base is a count - 635,000 overnight visitors in 2024,
from the prefectural survey - and the target is the town's own published
figure, 362 million yen of tourism spending in FY2023. Two further findings
support the direction of the proposal. First, revenue would not be heavily
skewed by season: read off the same monthly table, total visitors vary by 3.0x
across 2024 while overnight visitors move only 1.6x. Second, the tax base
itself is shrinking - overnight visitors are down 52% against 1990 - so the
longer the decision is deferred, the less the same rate will raise.


R2. "Spend it on holding prices up in September and October, not on filling the
     off-season with people."

This is the most strongly supported of the four recommendations, because two
independent sources point the same way. On price, the 2025 card data puts
weekday lodging spend at 20,436 yen in September, the cheapest month of the
year and 1.61x below January. On volume, the prefectural survey puts September
2024 at 96% of the median month and 1.31 times the quietest month; and for
overnight visitors specifically it ranks 9th of the 12 months, above the
median. September is not a month short of guests. It is a month short of
price - and these two facts come from separate datasets collected by different
organisations.

This is also a recommendation that depended on invalidating an existing figure.
Had we simply used the prefecture's "tourism spending per visitor" - 2,554 yen
in 2024 - the off-season problem would have appeared to be a volume problem. In
fact that figure is no more than the identity "overnight visitors x 10,500 yen
+ day visitors x 1,080 yen", which holds with zero error for every month and
year checked. It moves only with the composition of visitors and reflects
nothing about actual prices. Discovering this made the switch to card data
unavoidable, and it turned the substance of the recommendation around.


R3. "Design weekday policy around lodging and weekend policy around food and
     drink."

All the data shows is that two ratios sit on opposite sides of 1.0 - lodging at
0.947, food and beverage at 1.084 - calculated from a single year of card data
in which visitors are double-counted across the weekday and weekend split. That
is enough to argue that the two campaigns should not be bundled together, since
bundling them would have each cancel out part of the other. It is not enough to
determine the scale of either measure, and the site states as much on the face
of the chart.


R4. "Use part of the revenue to keep operators in business - and to start
     measuring occupancy."

According to the census and related statistics, accommodation and food service
establishments fell from 421 in 2006 to 235 in 2021. Their weight in the local
economy is equally clear: accommodation alone employs 1,128 people across 82
establishments - 13.8 per establishment against a town average of 7.4 - and
accounts for 13.5% of all employment in the town (23.2% for accommodation and
food services together). What the data does not support, on the other hand, is
that injecting subsidy would keep those businesses going, or the causal claim
that their closure is the direct cause of the decline in overnight visitors
rather than both arising from a common factor such as population decline.

The second half, on measuring occupancy, is on reflection the most strongly
supported recommendation on the whole site - precisely because it is drawn
directly from the fact of a data gap rather than from a claim about causation.
In every source we were able to check, no published occupancy figure exists for
Yugawara Town. And introducing an accommodation tax already places a monthly
reporting obligation on every operator for the number of guests staying. No
hypothesis about visitor behaviour has to hold true for this measure to be
worth pursuing.

One finding shapes the framing of the analysis without being tied directly to
any recommendation: the share of guests staying only one night has risen from
32.7% to 56.4%. It is the clearest indication that the problem lies in a loss
of density rather than in volume.


2. WHAT THE DATA CANNOT PROVE
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The revenue estimate is the figure most likely to stay with a reader, but it is
the least certain element on the site. Because no price elasticity data exists
for lodging in Yugawara, it assumes that demand does not respond to the tax at
all. The simulator therefore cannot reflect in its model the one effect a tax
increase is certain to produce. Every figure it produces is an upper bound.

Nor can this data prove the chain of causation the recommendations implicitly
depend on. What the data shows is that establishments are closing and that
overnight stays are falling. It does not show that either is the cause of the
other, and it is equally consistent with the same deterioration in demographics
producing both. Furthermore, the dataset contains nothing at all linking public
expenditure to any outcome, so R2's mechanism - that spending on off-season
positioning will raise off-season prices - is no more than a plausible
hypothesis, not something demonstrated.

The price series supporting R2 also covers only a single year, so it is a fact
about the present rather than a long-term trend. It cannot establish whether
the September discounting is something that has emerged recently, a stable
structural feature, or a widening one.
