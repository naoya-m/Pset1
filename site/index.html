Yugawara Tourism Policy Dashboard

Setting the rate of a proposed accommodation tax — from 17 primary statistical series, 1990–2026

The problem

If Yugawara introduces an accommodation tax, what rate closes the gap in its tourism budget?

Intended user: the Tourism Division of Yugawara Town and its Accommodation Tax Study Committee. Yugawara Town is considering an accommodation tax. The three numbers the decision turns on —— how fast the tax base is shrinking, how much each rate would raise, and how that compares with today's tourism budget —— sit in different statistical publications and cannot be read side by side. This dashboard puts all three on one screen so that a rate can actually be chosen.

Move the design assumptions below and the revenue estimate, the chart and the sensitivity table all update together. The overnight visitor counts behind the estimate are measured values from the Kanagawa Prefecture Tourist Visitor Survey (635,000 in 2024).

Recommendations

    The headline finding

    Visitor numbers have recovered. What has not recovered is the density of the stay and the capacity to host it

    Yugawara received 4,056,000 visitors in 2024, back to 99% of its 2019 level (4,096,000). That is usually where the analysis stops. Every other indicator points the other way, and the case for a tax depends on how that contradiction is read.

    Key findings

      Putting the indicators on one scale

      Indicators shown

      Indexed to the base year = 100

      One axis, so indicators with different units can be compared honestly (a second y-axis would invent a correlation that is not in the data). Below 100 means smaller than in the base year.

      Taking the structure apart

      Overnight and day visitors

      The total came back, but it came back on the day-trip side. The overnight share is 15.7% in 2024 against 15.8% in 1990 — the share held while the volume halved.

      Stays keep getting shorter

      The share of overnight guests who stay a single night, up from 32.7% in 2014 to 56.4% in 2025. Two- and three-night stays are being replaced by one-night stays.

      Compared with its neighbours — overnight share

      Three towns measured by the same prefectural survey on the same definition. Yugawara's overnight share stays consistently below Hakone's, and the gap is not closing.

      Capacity: accommodation and food service establishments

      From 421 in 2006 to 235 in 2021 — down 44% in 15 years. The businesses that would host additional visitors are disappearing faster than the visitors are returning.

      Workforce: population and ageing

      An ageing rate of 43.7% (2023) is 1.5–1.7 times Kanagawa Prefecture (25.8%) and Japan as a whole (29.0%). There are only 1.13 working-age residents per resident aged 65 or over.

      Access: daily boardings at JR Yugawara Station

      5,833 per day in FY2019, 5,008 in FY2022, 5,171 in FY2024. Non-pass boardings — broadly, visitors rather than commuters — have not returned to the FY2019 level.

      Evidence for how the revenue should be spent

      The off-season problem is not that nobody comes. It is that rooms are sold cheaply

      Measured spend per visitor from RESAS card data shows weekday lodging at ¥32,828 in January falling to ¥20,436 in September — a 1.61× spread across the year. Visitor numbers in September are not unusually low. What collapses is the price.

      The weekday/weekend split runs in opposite directions for the two largest spending categories. Lodging is worth more on weekdays (weekends are 5.3% cheaper), while food and beverage is worth 8.4% more at weekends. A single campaign covering both would work against itself.

      What this screen shows

      ▲September and October are when the price collapses
      Weekday lodging is ¥20,436 in September and ¥21,510 in October, just over 60% of January's ¥32,828. September is not a notably quiet month for visitor numbers, so this is a pricing problem, not a demand problem.
      ▲Weekday and weekend policy have to point in opposite directions
      Lodging is worth more on weekdays (weekend ÷ weekday = 0.947); food and beverage is worth more at weekends (1.084). Filling weekday rooms and growing weekend dining are two different programmes and need to be designed separately.
      ■The busiest months are the worst months for overnight stays
      April 2024 drew 636,000 visitors but only 7.7% of them stayed the night; June drew 554,000 at 7.4%. January was the quietest month (209,000) and the strongest for overnight share (26.3%). The seasonal swing is almost entirely day trippers — overnight visitors range only 41,000–64,000 across the year (1.6×) against 3.0× for the total.
      ■Read the direction of the gap, not its size
      In the RESAS weekday/weekend split, a visitor who pays on both a Friday and a Saturday is counted in both, so the levels come out lower than the headline spend per visitor. Only the direction of the difference is used as evidence here.
      Methodology

      How the data was collected, what the terms mean, and what this data cannot prove

      How the data was collected

      Everything comes from primary sources. No secondary citations, blogs or aggregator sites were used. Retrieved 2026-09-20. Nothing in the dataset can identify an individual: no names, contact details, medical information or precise personal locations.

      1. Machine extraction from published PDFs

      The Kanagawa Prefecture Tourist Visitor Survey and the Yugawara Town Statistical Handbook are published only as PDFs. Their text layer was extracted with PDFKit (Swift, macOS) and reshaped into tables in Python. Two tables whose layout does not survive extraction (Tables 4 and 5 of the prefectural survey) were transcribed against rendered page images instead.

      2. Verification by identity

      Every extracted year was checked against three identities: annual total = sum of 12 months, total visitors = overnight + day, and total spend = sum of its components. The annual figures rebuilt from the monthly table were then cross-checked against two other published tables and against the figures in the town's own accommodation tax study report; all agreed.

      3. Transcription from screen recordings (RESAS)

      RESAS offers no CSV export and no public API for these views. The screen was recorded while the pointer traced every month; frames were cut at 0.5-second intervals, the tooltip was detected and cropped automatically, and the values were read off. About a minute of recording yields twelve months of one series.

      4. Joining two sources into one series

      Years 2005–2009 come from the town handbook and 2010–2024 from the prefectural survey. The two overlap in 2015 and agree exactly (3,109k total / 568k overnight / 2,541k day), so they are treated as a single series.

      ⚠ The most important caveat — the prefecture's "tourism spending" is not a measured figure

      ▲It is visitor counts multiplied by a fixed rate

      The following identity holds with zero error for every month and every year from 2019 to 2024:

      tourism spending = overnight visitors × ¥10,500 + day visitors × ¥1,080

      The figure therefore carries no information about actual spending behaviour: it moves only when the mix of overnight and day visitors moves. Anything derived from it is labelled estimated on this site, and measured spend per visitor from RESAS card data is labelled measured. The two are defined differently and their levels must not be compared (¥10,500 against roughly ¥28,000).

      Definitions and units

      Missing data, bias and uncertainty — what this data cannot prove

      Indicators that could not be obtained

      • Room occupancy rate. Not published for Yugawara Town on its own, and absent from the RESAS overnight guest analysis. No primary figure exists. Consequence: the tax base cannot be cross-checked from the supply side (rooms × occupancy); it rests on the visitor-count side alone.
      • Guest nights from the national Accommodation Survey. Municipal-level publication is limited. The RESAS overnight guest analysis was tested as a substitute and rejected: its year-on-year direction disagrees with the prefectural survey in 7 of 11 years, and the ratio between the two wanders between 1.01 and 1.59 with no stable conversion factor. It is used here only as a share within its own source (the one-night share).
      • The distribution of room rates. Without it there is no way to estimate how much of the base an exemption threshold would remove. That is why "share of nights taxed" in the simulator is an assumption the user sets rather than a derived figure.
      • Accommodation establishment counts after 2016. The town handbook stops there.
      • Road traffic census, bus GTFS, bike-share GBFS, search trends and review data. Out of scope here, either because acquisition requires large GIS files and periodic feeds that do not belong in a static dataset, or because scraping would conflict with the providers' terms.

      Known biases

      • RESAS card data covers card-present transactions only. Payments made in advance through online travel agencies are excluded, which probably understates lodging spend. The figures are estimates derived from Visa transactions.
      • The weekday/weekend split double-counts visitors. Somebody who pays on a Friday and again on a Saturday is counted in both, so these levels sit below the headline spend per visitor. Only the direction of the difference is used as evidence on this site; the size of the difference is not.
      • Visitor counts are visits, not people. One person visiting three surveyed points is counted three times. Because the number and choice of survey points differ by municipality, the comparison with neighbouring towns is kept to shares rather than absolute levels.
      • Population comes in three incompatible series — the Basic Resident Register (1 Jan), the prefectural Population by Age Survey (1 Jan) and the Population Census (1 Oct). They are never mixed here, and each chart states which one it uses.
      • 2020–2022 fall inside the pandemic. Any trend line spanning those years cannot separate structural change from a temporary shock.

      Limits of the simulator

      • The revenue estimate assumes zero price elasticity of demand: it does not model visitors deciding not to come because of the tax. In reality, the higher the rate the further actual revenue will fall below the estimate. Read every figure as an upper bound.
      • The share of nights taxed, the collection cost and the average room rate are assumptions set by the user, not statistics. They are marked assumption wherever they appear.
      • The −1% and −3% scenarios are straight extrapolations of the past trend. They are not forecasts.
